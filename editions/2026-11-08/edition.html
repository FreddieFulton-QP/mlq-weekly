01
Nvidia's board opens an independent probe into Jensen Huang
CBS News, Nov 8, 2026 · corroborated by CNN, Axios, Tom's Hardware

What happened: Nvidia's board announced Sunday Nov 8 it is launching an independent investigation of CEO Jensen Huang, run by independent directors with outside counsel, after US intelligence reportedly concluded that Alice Huang (no relation), a Singapore-based Megaspeed executive and major Nvidia chip buyer, was relaying information to China's Ministry of State Security, and that Jensen Huang had a "close relationship" with her. Huang separately admitted to an extramarital affair with her and apologised to his family, the board and shareholders. The board says it has no reason to believe trade secrets or US national security were compromised and that Huang retains its support; three House committee chairs have separately demanded a closed-door briefing, now set for Nov 18 — one day after Nvidia's Nov 17 earnings.

Why it matters: Yale's Jeffrey Sonnenfeld puts Huang's odds of keeping the job at "about 50-50" — and because Huang holds no super-voting stock, an exit is mechanically easier to force than at most AI-adjacent founder-controlled companies. Nvidia underwrites a huge share of the compute commitments behind OpenAI, Anthropic, xAI and the hyperscalers; a leadership vacuum there is a systemic risk to the whole AI financing chain, not just one company's stock.

02
Voters hand the AI build-out its first real electoral defeat
baxtel.com (citing Ballotpedia/CNBC/Data Center Dynamics/Washington Post), Nov 4, 2026

What happened: At the Nov 3 US midterms, voters in 10 states decided 41 local data-center ballot measures — and backed restrictions or outright bans in 40 of them. Ohio alone had 21 measures; Granville banned data centers 82-18%, Defiance capped new capacity above 25MW, Ashville rejected two EdgeConneX term sheets. A CNBC poll found 56% of voters called data centers a crucial or major factor in their Congressional vote. Democrats also took Congress, with several incoming governors having campaigned explicitly on data-center accountability.

Why it matters: This compounded with weak chip earnings and rate concerns into a brutal Nov 5 sell-off (Section 09) — the Nasdaq's worst day in 19 months. Politically, "AI infrastructure" has gone from an abstract national-competitiveness talking point to a concrete, local, votable grievance in a matter of one election cycle.

03
Anthropic's S-1/A: "we may be imminently approaching" self-improving AI
TechCrunch, Nov 2, 2026, citing Anthropic's revised S-1/A filing

What happened: Anthropic's amended IPO filing says the company may be "imminently" approaching the threshold its own Responsible Scaling Policy defines as dramatically accelerated, AI-driven R&D ("recursive self-improvement") — and admits it has historically only identified such acceleration after the fact. No model is currently designated as having crossed the line, and Anthropic says it won't let Claude autonomously design successor models without meaningful human oversight; it's considering RSP updates to formalise that.

Why it matters: A securities filing is partly a marketing document, so some skepticism is warranted — but a frontier lab publicly saying it isn't fully sure it would notice its own acceleration in real time is a genuinely new kind of admission, not boilerplate risk-factor language. Claude's coding, math and cyber gains this year make the claim harder to dismiss outright.

04
Quant firms formalise the "AI enablement" job — and it's spreading fast
Tower Research Capital careers site & LinkedIn, Nov 3–4, 2026

What happened: In the same week it poached a 12-year Millennium veteran to run mid-frequency trading strategy and ran a firm-wide internal AI hackathon, Tower Research Capital posted a dedicated "AI Enablement Lead" role to formally drive AI-tool adoption across its engineering and research org. IMC Trading posted a near-identical "Software Engineer, AI Tooling" role the same week — a job category that didn't really exist by that name a month ago.

Why it matters: This is a genuinely new title, distinct from both "quant researcher" and "ML research scientist" — someone whose job is governance, adoption measurement and vendor management for agentic AI tools across an entire firm. See Section 07 for the cross-firm pattern.

05
OpenAI: a cyber-model arms race, a fresh $11bn tranche, and a 2027 IPO hint
VentureBeat / TechCrunch, Nov 5–6, 2026; valuation figure via Bloomberg republish

What happened: OpenAI shipped GPT-6 Cyber on Nov 5 — its first model rated "Critical" cyber capability under its own Preparedness Framework, priced roughly double Anthropic's comparable model and five times Google's — gated behind an expanded "Daybreak Red for Critical Infrastructure" program. Separately, OpenAI reportedly closed an initial $11bn tranche of a new round at a $1.25 trillion pre-money valuation, and president Greg Brockman said the company could be "IPO ready" in 2027.

Why it matters: A dedicated "Critical" cyber-capability tier is a new rung on the capability ladder that didn't publicly exist a month ago, and it's being commercialised, not just red-teamed internally. The pricing gap between OpenAI, Anthropic and Google on the same capability class is itself a signal of how each lab is positioning cyber-offense research commercially.

Meta buys Conductor, the Mac app that runs several coding agents at once

08
"Sparks of Weight-Space Programming": an LLM edits another model's weights directly
MIT CSAIL, Nov 2, 2026 (via secondary write-up; numbers independently reproduced)

What happened: Researchers let GPT-6 Astra directly edit the weight tensors of a small open model (Qwen3.5-0.8B) that kept getting stuck in repetitive "thinking loops" on math problems. Astra diagnosed the cause as a specific direction being written into two layers' output, then zeroed it out across just 131,072 of the model's roughly 800 million weights (0.016%) — lifting MATH-500 accuracy from 7.9% to 48.1%.

Why it matters: Full technical write-up and the important caveats are in Sections 05 and 10.

09
Goldman Sachs pulls Claude Code and GitHub from traders and bankers
Bloomberg, Nov 4, 2026, via secondary reporting

What happened: Goldman Sachs updated internal guidance restricting most traders and bankers from using "advanced" AI coding tools — Claude Code and GitHub by name — limiting access to its engineering workforce (roughly 12,000 people) and to "strats" (staff who blend trading, quant research and software engineering). A spokesperson said the move follows a review of productivity and risk management; the bank's CIO had publicly praised traders' and bankers' "vibe coding" as recently as July.

Why it matters: A major bank is drawing a harder line on who gets agentic coding tools at the exact moment quant trading firms are doing the opposite — formalising dedicated roles to spread AI-tool access firm-wide (item 04 above, Section 06). Five threads dominated this week: a governance crisis at the company that underwrites the compute build-out, a lab publicly flagging its own acceleration risk, a cyber-capability arms race, an acquisition spree for agentic-coding know-how, and the first electoral check on all of it.

ANvidia's governance crisis becomes a systemic AI-industry risk

After an Oct 29 New York Times report that US intelligence believes Megaspeed executive Alice Huang was relaying information to Beijing and had a "close relationship" with Jensen Huang, this week brought: three House committee chairs demanding a closed-door briefing (Nov 6, now set for Nov 18); Nvidia's board announcing an independent investigation (Nov 8) after Huang admitted to an affair with her; and Yale's Jeffrey Sonnenfeld putting Huang's odds of retaining the role at "about 50-50," naming Eric Schmidt as a plausible successor. Research Radar

arXiv's Monday listing carries papers received Thursday–Friday of the prior week, so several items below show a Nov 5–6 submission timestamp despite surfacing in the Nov 9 listing.

Tap any headline to expand it.

01Scientist World: Grow a Research World for AI-ScientistsarXiv 2611.08478, Princeton University, Nov 5, 2026

What it is: An automated pipeline that turns 1,000 research papers into executable environments — extracting each paper's open question, reproducing the original authors' method as a baseline, and letting an AI "scientist" be benchmarked directly against what the humans achieved.

What it solves: The literature is readable but not "runnable" — there was no way to directly test whether an AI's proposed method actually beats a real, reproduced human baseline at scale.

Result: Frontier agents reportedly beat the original human-authored results in 74% of the 1,000 cases; training a small model on these environments lifted its research-ideation performance 158%, matching frontier-model performance.

Why it's interesting: Close to a blueprint for automated strategy-discovery environments more broadly — generating and testing new hypotheses against reproducible baselines, generalised here from physical-science papers.

02Muir: Linear Attention with Sensitivity-Allocated Gating and Matched-Filter ReadoutarXiv 2611.08376, Google (per secondary reporting), Nov 5, 2026

What it is: A linear-attention architecture (constant-size state instead of a growing KV cache) that fixes two specific inefficiencies in a strong prior design (Gated DeltaNet-2): an over-parameterised "erase" gate, and a tendency for slow-decaying state rows to dominate the readout and drown out other signal.

How it works: A cheaper, lower-rank erase gate; a new matched-filter readout gate that stops dominant rows from swamping the rest; the freed parameters reinvested into a wider feed-forward network; a parallel-scan update stable at large chunk sizes.

Result: At 1.34B parameters, cuts perplexity 1.8–2.0% vs. GDN-2 and 2.5–3.6% vs. a comparable Transformer; generalises from 4K training context to 10M-token streams; trains 2.7x faster than a Transformer at 32K context.

03Unpacking the Compute Efficiency of Strong-to-Weak On-Policy Distillation against GRPOarXiv 2611.08453, Salesforce AI Research / UIUC, Nov 5, 2026

What it is: A controlled, apples-to-apples comparison of two dominant post-training recipes — reinforcement-learning fine-tuning (GRPO) and on-policy distillation (OPD) — measured as held-out accuracy per training FLOP.

Result: When the "teacher" is just the student's own earlier GRPO checkpoint, OPD recovers that checkpoint's accuracy with roughly 50x fewer FLOPs than running GRPO from scratch; consolidating several domain-specialist checkpoints into one generalist student via multi-teacher OPD costs only 5–8% of the specialists' original training cost.

Why it's interesting: A rare apples-to-apples compute-efficiency audit of the two dominant post-training recipes, with a concrete, actionable recipe: train specialists with RL, then consolidate cheaply via distillation.

04A Behavioural Framework for Predicting and Understanding Loss of Control in Frontier AI SystemsarXiv 2611.07953, Tsinghua University (College of AI) & Shanghai Qi Zhi Institute & University of Cambridge, Nov 5, 2026

What it is: A framework that splits "loss of control" risk into three measurable factors — misaligned motive, harm-enabling capability, and monitoring evasion — operationalised via 13 aspects and 29 evaluation tasks into a single score.

Result: Tested across 13 frontier models, the score correlated strongly (Pearson r = 0.84) with actual failure rates in contained long-horizon risk scenarios, and risk varied systematically between models independent of raw capability; targeted fine-tuning based on diagnosed weak points reduced failure rates without hurting general performance.

Why it's interesting: One of the more rigorous, falsifiable attempts to turn "AI loss of control" from a philosophical worry into a measured, model-comparable score.

05CodeQuant2: Enhanced Outlier Mitigation and Adaptive Clustering for Low-Precision Reasoning ModelsarXiv 2611.09868, New York University, Nov 6, 2026

What it is: A clustering-based lookup-table quantization scheme for reasoning models, using rotation/scaling to tame activation outliers that standard calibration misses because they occur in the very first decoding steps.

Result: Accuracy gains on reasoning benchmarks plus up to 2.85x inference speedup over BF16 — a meaningful win on the cost side of deploying reasoning models at scale.

06FARR: Forecast-Aware Residual Refinement with Matched Endpoint Evidence for Long-Horizon Time-Series ForecastingarXiv 2611.07870, institution unconfirmed, Nov 5, 2026

What it is: A model-agnostic wrapper that measures how much an auxiliary conditioning signal (extra context fed into a forecaster) is distorting a forecast, by comparing predictions with and without it, then applies a horizon-wise gated correction.

Result: Beat baselines on dataset-average MSE/MAE across four long-horizon benchmarks — a clean, cheap diagnostic-and-correction wrapper rather than a new architecture.

07Representational drift in a code modelarXiv 2611.09204, cs.CL, ~Nov 5–6, 2026 (institution not independently verified)

What it is: An interpretability study of how a code model's output behaviour stays stable over training even as which internal neurons carry that behaviour keeps shifting — a direct analogue to representational drift observed in biological neural systems.

Why it's interesting: A reminder that interpretability tools built against a fixed set of "meaningful neurons" can go stale even when the model's behaviour hasn't changed.

New to ML? New to Trading?

The rest of this newsletter assumes you already work in the field. This section doesn't — it's written for anyone curious about where machine learning and quantitative trading meet, whether you're a student, early in your career, or just reading in from the outside.

New to ML

Three ideas behind this week's AI news.

New to ML · Model editing
Weight-space programming

The idea: Normally, fixing a bug in a neural network means retraining it — gradient descent, loss functions, real compute spend. Weight-space programming is a different idea: give a capable AI model direct read/write access to a smaller model's raw internal numbers, the way a human might patch a compiled program, rather than retraining from examples. This week, researchers at MIT had one AI model diagnose why a smaller model kept getting stuck in repetitive "thinking loops" on math problems, then make a tiny, surgical edit — touching just 0.016% of its internal numbers — that lifted its accuracy on a standard math test roughly sixfold.

The catch: This wasn't the model "learning math." It removed an obstacle that was masking ability already there, rather than adding new ability. The underlying technique isn't new either — what's new is that an AI model did the diagnosis and the fix itself, with no retraining loop at all.

Think of it like this: instead of re-teaching a student from scratch, a tutor finds the one specific bad habit that's wasting all their time on every problem, and corrects just that.
New to ML · Model architecture
Linear attention and the "matched-filter" problem

The idea: A standard AI language model keeps a growing record of every past word it has seen, which makes very long documents expensive to process. "Linear attention" models instead compress everything seen so far into one fixed-size summary — cheaper, but historically worse quality. This week's research found that in a strong existing design, a few overly dominant pieces of that compressed summary were drowning out everything else useful, while the mechanism for actively forgetting old information was needlessly bulky. Fixing both let the model handle documents two thousand times longer than it was trained on, while training almost three times faster.

The catch: This is an efficiency and engineering improvement, not a new capability — but efficiency improvements like this are what make long-document AI assistants cheap enough to actually use at scale.

Think of it like this: if you're compressing a whole book into a one-page summary, you need a rule that stops one dramatic chapter from crowding every other chapter off the page.
New to ML · Training methods
On-policy distillation: a cheaper way to improve a model

The idea: Reinforcement learning improves a model by having it try tasks, score its own attempts, and adjust — expensive, because every improvement requires fresh trial-and-error. "On-policy distillation" instead has a student model generate its own attempts, then learn from a frozen, already-good teacher model's feedback on those same attempts, without a full trial-and-error loop. Researchers this week found that when the teacher is simply an earlier, already-trained version of the same model, this approach can match that same performance using roughly 50 times less compute.

The catch: It can't teach a model something genuinely new that no teacher knows — it's a way of transferring and consolidating existing skill cheaply, not a route to new capability on its own.

Think of it like this: instead of re-running an entire experiment to improve it, you have a known-good version grade your own attempt and tell you what to adjust.

New to Trading

This week's concept: Derivatives.

Derivatives

A derivative is a contract whose value is derived from the price of something else — a stock, an index, a currency, an interest rate. The three workhorse forms are options (the right, not the obligation, to buy or sell at a set price), futures (an obligation to transact at a set price on a future date), and swaps (an exchange of cash flows).

How it works: An option's value depends on the underlying's price, how volatile that price is expected to be, time to expiry, and interest rates. Traders track the "Greeks": delta (sensitivity to the underlying's price), gamma (how fast delta itself changes), vega (sensitivity to volatility), and theta (value lost as time passes). A market maker in options isn't betting on direction — it continuously rebalances its position in the underlying to stay roughly delta-neutral, earning its profit from the bid-ask spread and from selling volatility at a premium to what actually plays out.

A simple example: A firm sells a call option on a stock, collecting a premium. To hedge, it buys some of the underlying stock (an amount set by the option's delta). As the stock price moves, delta changes, so the firm continuously buys or sells more stock to stay hedged — this continuous rebalancing is itself a trading strategy with its own costs and risks, distinct from simply predicting whether the stock goes up or down.

Why trading firms care: Derivatives let a firm take a view on volatility itself, not just direction; they provide leverage; and they let risk be sliced and transferred precisely. For trading firms, derivatives markets are often deeper and more liquid testing grounds for pricing models than the underlying cash markets themselves.

Where ML fits in: Classical derivatives pricing relies on simplifying assumptions that real markets violate. Machine-learning models now learn volatility surfaces directly from market data rather than fitting a parametric model to them; neural networks price complex, path-dependent derivatives far faster than traditional numerical methods once trained; and reinforcement learning is increasingly used to learn hedging strategies directly from data, accounting for real transaction costs that classical formulas assume away.
