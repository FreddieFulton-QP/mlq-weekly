01
Jensen Huang resigns as Nvidia's CEO amid an alleged China-spy-affair scandal
Nvidia Newsroom, Nov 12, 2026 · corroborated by BBC, NPR, CNBC, Reuters

What happened: Nvidia's board announced Huang resigned as President, CEO and board member "effective immediately" on Nov 12, following the board's independent investigation, announced Nov 8, into reports that Huang was the target of a foreign-intelligence effort. CFO Colette Kress becomes interim CEO (keeping the CFO title); Harvey Jones, former Synopsys CEO, becomes Nvidia's first-ever independent board chair. The underlying allegations, per the New York Times and widely corroborated: Huang had an affair with Alice Huang (no relation), a Singapore-based executive at Nvidia customer Megaspeed whom US intelligence believes relayed information to China and helped facilitate at least $7.5bn in Nvidia chip shipments there; a second reported affair was with an Nvidia VP who reported to him. Independent directors and a former federal prosecutor are continuing the investigation despite the resignation.

Why it matters: This is the largest governance shock in frontier-AI infrastructure history. Huang had 60+ direct reports and no groomed successor; Nvidia underwrites a vast share of the compute commitments behind every lab in this newsletter. Congress has summoned Huang and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick to testify in December over China chip-export enforcement; Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said on Nov 15 the board should hire an outside CEO for "a dramatic shake-up," and a Reuters exclusive found $3bn+ in Chinese computing projects built around export-restricted Nvidia processors. Trump reportedly called Huang "an honorable guy" and floated Tim Cook as a replacement; a "Bring Jensen Back" open letter drew 150,000+ signatures including Elon Musk.

Anthropic targets a $1.4tn fully diluted valuation as its IPO approaches, reportedly oversubscribed up to 7x
(roadshow/range/filings) (oversubscription multiple). Anthropic newsroom & S-1/A, Nov 10; CNBC, TechCrunch, Nov 10–15

What happened: Anthropic launched its IPO roadshow Nov 10, setting a $70–$78/share range for 825m Class A shares (implying up to $1.405tn fully-diluted valuation, up from $965bn in May). Pricing and the Nasdaq debut ("ANT") were tracking to Nov 18–19, just after this window; by Nov 15 the deal was reported oversubscribed sixfold-to-sevenfold. Seven chip/cloud partners — Google, Nvidia, AMD, Microsoft, Broadcom, Micron, TSMC — agreed to buy $26bn of non-voting Class N shares in a concurrent private placement. Q3 revenue was $17bn (nine-month revenue $33.2bn); nine-month net loss $104bn; run-rate revenue hit $81bn by end-October; compute/infrastructure commitments exceed $900bn; founders retain 50.1% voting control via a Class F share and Long-Term Benefit Trust.

Why it matters: The first frontier-lab IPO in history, approaching pricing in the same week Nvidia lost its CEO — a genuine stress test of whether public markets still believe the AI capex story. It is also the backdrop against which XTX Markets founder Alex Gerko's early Anthropic stake could be worth up to $3.5bn (Section 06).

03
Claude Fable & Mythos 5.2 launch — Anthropic's first new frontier models since the "slow down" call
Anthropic, Nov 9, 2026 · CNBC, Nov 9

What happened: Anthropic shipped Claude Fable 5.2 (broadly available) and Claude Mythos 5.2 (gated to vetted organisations via Life Sciences/Cyber Verification programs) — 30% faster and 20% cheaper than the prior generation ($8/$40 per million input/output tokens). Pre-release claims include formalising Fermat's Last Theorem in 30 hours and designing three working electronic devices with minimal human guidance (in partnership with Diode Computers). Same week: Anthropic launched Claude Meetings, Whiteboards, Sheets and a GA'd Claude Data, explicitly courting white-collar knowledge workers ahead of the IPO; and disclosed "Anvil," an automated red-teaming system that found a Chrome/Firefox JS-engine jailbreak and a bio-safeguard jailbreak faster than human red-teamers.

Why it's technically interesting: Full write-ups in Sections 04 and 10.

04
Hedge funds slash risk at the fastest pace in over a decade — while the market rallied
Bloomberg, citing a Morgan Stanley prime-brokerage note, Nov 11, 2026

What happened: Global long-short equity hedge funds cut book size 10.7% since late June, with the first week of November marking the fastest reduction in gross-plus-net exposure in more than a decade. Asia-based funds (especially Taiwan, South Korea) took the heaviest tech cuts; only Hong Kong/China funds were net buyers. Separately, LPL/Goldman prime data shows hedge funds turned net underweight the broad AI trade for the first time since early 2023 — mostly de-risking (selling existing longs), not fresh short-selling. Yet the S&P 500 rose 2.5% and the Nasdaq 2.3% over the same week, their best week since August.

Why it matters: A real divergence between positioning and price — funds are quietly reducing AI-trade risk even as the index-level tape looks healthy, which Section 09 argues is best read as a crowding/concentration story, not a verdict that the AI trade is "broken."

05
DeepMind formally verifies the Signal protocol, headlining Google's biggest model week of the year
Google / Google DeepMind, Nov 10–12, 2026

06
Point72 alleges a quant used Claude Code and Codex to steal a colleague's research before jumping to a rival
(court filings). eFinancialCareers, Nov 11–12, 2026

What happened: Xin Jin, a research analyst at Cubist (Point72's systematic arm) since 2019, told the firm on Sept 16 he'd accepted a rival's offer. Two days later Cubist fired him for cause, alleging he used Claude Code and OpenAI Codex (4,117 queries, Feb 28–May 31, 2026) to extract and synthesise a colleague's proprietary research and source code for use at the new firm. Jin disputes the allegations in his own affidavit (filed Nov 11) and says Cubist owes him $30m+ in withheld compensation; his new role doesn't start until June 2028 under a non-compete.

Why it matters: The first case this newsletter has tracked of a quant firm alleging an employee used agentic coding tools specifically to exfiltrate IP ahead of a move — a governance story every quant-firm compliance and legal team will now be watching, and a cautionary tale for any candidate currently mid-move with firm-owned AI-tool logs. CoreWeave's earnings beat sparks a "neocloud" rally, easing AI-debt-bubble fears mid-crisis
CoreWeave investor relations, Nov 10–11, 2026

What happened: CoreWeave reported Q3 revenue of $3.66bn (up 168% YoY, beating consensus), raised FY26 revenue guidance to $13.0–13.3bn, and said it does not plan to tap unsecured debt markets through 2027 — even as its Q3 net loss widened to $709m and its interest bill kept climbing on a mounting debt pile — all after its 2032 bonds had spiked toward distressed-debt yields on AI-financing-bubble fears. 09
Jane Street reveals it more than doubled its Nvidia holdings
Fortune, Nov 12, 2026, citing Q3 13F filings

What happened: Jane Street's Q3 13F filing, made public on Nov 12 (the same day Huang resigned) but reporting holdings as of Sept 30 — roughly six weeks earlier — showed the market-making giant held approximately 23.8 million Nvidia shares worth $5.44bn, up about 102% from its prior quarter's disclosed position.

Why it matters: A genuine quant-specific angle on the week's biggest AI story, and a concrete illustration of how deeply even the most sophisticated market-making firms had leaned into the Nvidia trade heading into the governance shock. Two important caveats: the Sept 30 snapshot predates the resignation by weeks, so this isn't evidence of a trade made "days before"; and a 13F is a backward-looking disclosure that says nothing about current exposure, hedges or options positions — a large long position isn't necessarily an unhedged bet, and it shouldn't be read as evidence Jane Street is sitting on a large loss.

10
A major Microsoft 365 outage knocks out Outlook, Teams and Copilot for a day and a half
Microsoft's own status updates, via Wikipedia's incident summary; BBC, NBC News, Al Jazeera, Nov 12–14, 2026

What happened: Starting around 17:00–18:00 UTC on Nov 12, Microsoft 365 suffered a worldwide outage knocking out Outlook, Teams, Copilot and Exchange Online mailboxes for many of its roughly 430 million users, with problems reported in more than 70 countries, including government bodies in the US, Australia, Japan, the Philippines, Singapore and South Africa, plus the UK's NHS and Toronto-area hospitals. Some servers came back by 23:04 UTC that night, with partial recovery reported around 04:36 UTC on Nov 13 and services not fully restored until the morning of Nov 14. Microsoft attributed it to settings applied to servers after an update — a process that establishes secure connections became overwhelmed and stopped handling requests — and said it was not a cyberattack or related to AI agents; the incident followed that month's Patch Tuesday update, which covered 1,094 vulnerabilities.

Why it matters: One of the broadest enterprise-software outages of the year, landing in the same week as Nvidia's leadership crisis and Anthropic's IPO push — a reminder that AI-era reliability risk isn't confined to the frontier labs themselves. The Anderson Economic Group estimated roughly $7bn in lost productivity; UK minister Luke Pollard called for "greater resilience" against this kind of technology failure.

AI & ML: Week in Review

Five threads dominated: a leadership implosion at the company underwriting the compute build-out, a lab IPO that turned into a referendum on the whole AI trade, the biggest model week of the year from one lab, two credible open-weight releases from two others, and a quiet repositioning by the hedge-fund community even as headline indices rallied.

Tap any headline to expand it.

ANvidia's leadership implodes as the board begins its search for Huang's replacement

What began Nov 8 as a board investigation into reports Jensen Huang was "the target of a foreign intelligence effort" escalated fast: by Nov 12 Huang had resigned as President, CEO and director, "effective immediately." The underlying allegations — an affair with Megaspeed executive Alice Huang, alleged by US intelligence to have relayed information to China and facilitated at least $7.5bn in chip shipments there, plus a second reported affair with an Nvidia VP who reported to him — are reported via the New York Times and widely corroborated, not yet independently confirmed by Nvidia itself. CFO Colette Kress is interim CEO; Harvey Jones (ex-Synopsys) is the new independent board chair; the board says it has "not officially contacted any potential candidates" for a permanent replacement and is still selecting a search firm. Succession speculation centres on CUDA inventor Ian Buck, the prediction-market favourite among internal candidates, and Arm's Rene Haas — the only external name with any credible reporting (a single FT story) behind it; a handful of other outside names have been floated by analysts with no confirmation any of them have actually been approached.

Congress has summoned Huang and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick to testify in December over China chip-export enforcement; Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said on Nov 15 the more important question is whether Nvidia "knowingly" let advanced chips reach China via shell companies, and urged the board toward an outside CEO hire. A Reuters exclusive (Nov 13) identified $3bn+ in Chinese computing projects built around export-restricted Nvidia processors; TechCrunch reported (Nov 13) that researchers found fingerprints of export-banned Blackwell-GPU training in several open-weight Chinese models. Trump reportedly called Huang "an honorable guy" and discussed the matter with him before the resignation; a "Bring Jensen Back" open letter has drawn 150,000+ signatures, and one analyst (Deepwater's Gene Munster) now puts the odds of a Huang return at 51%, up from 10% the prior Friday — a genuinely live possibility, not settled.

BAnthropic's IPO becomes a referendum on the whole AI trade

Anthropic launched its roadshow Nov 10 at a $70–$78/share range, targeting up to a $1.405tn fully-diluted valuation (not yet priced as this edition closes), with pricing/debut tracking to Nov 18–19. By Nov 15 the deal was reported oversubscribed sixfold-to-sevenfold — still developing as this edition closes. Seven chip/cloud partners (Google, Nvidia, AMD, Microsoft, Broadcom, Micron, TSMC) committed $26bn to a concurrent non-voting private placement; Google and Nvidia each took the largest tranches (~$10bn apiece). The S-1/A shows Q3 revenue of $17bn, nine-month net loss of $104bn, run-rate revenue of $81bn as of Oct 31 (up $10bn in a single month), and $900bn+ of outstanding compute commitments, including $110bn/10yr with Amazon and $125–150bn for Google chips starting 2028. Founders keep 50.1% voting control via a Class F share and the Long-Term Benefit Trust — public buyers get economic exposure, not governance influence.

The same week, Anthropic shipped Claude Fable and Mythos 5.2 (its first new frontier models since Dario Amodei's September call to slow the pace of development), plus Claude Meetings/Whiteboards/Sheets and a GA'd Claude Data — a visible pivot toward white-collar knowledge-work tools timed deliberately ahead of the IPO (Section 04).

CGoogle's biggest model week of the year

Google GA'd Gemini 4 Carbon (Nov 10) — a frontier model for cybersecurity and long-horizon agentic work, #1 on CursorBench 4.0, Perplexity's WANDR and Zapier's AutomationBench at more than half the cost of the next-best external model — then Gemini 4 Hydrogen (Nov 12), a fast/cheap tier replacing the "Flash" branding that already surpasses the prior generation's default tier on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index. DeepMind's AlphaProof Code (Nov 10) used Carbon-powered multi-agent frameworks to generate 6m+ lines of Lean proof code formally verifying key security properties of the Signal messaging protocol's actual implementation — a "grand challenge" in software security done in four weeks instead of years, with only 1% of the codebase flagged for human review: the first time a widely-deployed cryptographic protocol has been formally verified at this depth, by any method. Also this week: Gemini Robotics 2.5 (96% pick/pack success on a Mercedes-Benz production line using Apptronik's Apollo 2 humanoid) and a new Gemini Decisions API for high-throughput structured classification.

Why it's technically interesting: AlphaProof Code is a genuinely different kind of result from a benchmark win — it's an end-to-end, verifiable engineering deliverable (a real protocol, formally proven) rather than a score on a held-out test set, and it's a strong signal of how fast agentic formal-verification tooling is maturing. Full teaching note in Section 10.

DAn open-weight week: two credible non-Chinese frontier releases

Mistral open-weighted Mistral Large 4 ("Le Chonk," 1T-parameter MoE, 52B active, Apache 2.0) on Nov 9, pretrained on 3,840 Nvidia GB200 GPUs over 45 days in its own Paris data centre, scoring 41% on Terminal-Bench 4.0 after three additional weeks of RL. Three days later, Mira Murati's Thinking Machines Lab published open weights for Inkling 2 Small (594B MoE, 17B active, natively multimodal, 1M context) — described by several outlets as the strongest open model not trained in China, scoring 86.3% on SWE-Bench Pro v2 (Hard) and integrated same-week with Unsloth, SGLang, vLLM and Snowflake Cortex. One independent evaluator (orcarouter.ai) flagged that Thinking Machines' promised external safety-testing report had not yet been published at the time weights went out — a fair open question for a safety-literate readership.

EHedge funds quietly de-risk from AI even as the tape rallies

Per a Morgan Stanley prime-brokerage note reported by Bloomberg on Nov 11, global long-short equity hedge funds cut book size 10.7% since late June — a reduction that had been building for months but hit its fastest pace in more than a decade during the first week of November; separately, LPL/Goldman prime data shows funds turned net underweight the broad AI trade for the first time since early 2023, mostly through selling existing longs rather than fresh shorting. Yet the S&P 500 rose 2.5% and the Nasdaq 2.3% the same week — their best week since August, helped substantially by CoreWeave's earnings-driven "neocloud" rally (Section 09). Full market detail, including Nvidia's own divergence from the wider chip sector, is in Section 09.

Why it's interesting: A genuine positioning/price divergence — funds cutting risk while the index rallies is consistent with concentrated, crowded AI positions being unwound in an otherwise broadening market, rather than with a broad loss of conviction in AI itself.

Research Radar

A strong week for quant-finance-relevant papers specifically — four of the nine below have a direct trading-desk application, not just a general-ML one.

Tap any headline to expand it.

01Market-Conditioned JEPA: Learning from Unpredictable Market ShocksarXiv 2611.13572, University of Hong Kong (one author also affiliated with Grace Investment Machine, a fund), Nov 10, 2026

What it is: A joint-embedding predictive architecture (JEPA) adapted to financial time series, conditioning each asset's predicted future embedding on a "leave-one-out" market factor built from every other asset's future embeddings.

What it solves: Per-asset self-supervised models treat market-wide shocks as pure noise; a formal latent-factor argument shows conditioning on realised cross-sectional market behaviour both makes market exposure a necessary predictive input and reduces noise in the asset-specific signal.

Result: On 11 years of minute-level Chinese A-share data, beats per-asset JEPA and existing time-series foundation models across 9 targets (returns, exposure, risk, liquidity); best Sharpe/drawdown in a historical backtest; learned factor loadings rank stocks by market beta almost as well as a supervised probe, with zero beta labels.

Why a quant firm should care: A genuine return/risk/liquidity forecasting architecture benchmarked with an actual Sharpe/drawdown backtest on real equity microstructure data, plus a label-free beta-recovery result directly useful for risk-model construction.

02Per-market nonlinear factor models for seasonality and time-series momentumarXiv 2611.15657 & 2611.15646, ReSolve Asset Management, Nov 11, 2026

What it is: Two companion papers from a named systematic asset manager fitting nonlinear statistical-learning models separately for each individual futures market (bonds, commodities, equities, FX), rather than one pooled global response curve, for (a) seasonality anomalies and (b) classic time-series momentum.

Result: Five seasonality anomalies are pervasive across asset classes; slow-moving seasonal patterns survive transaction costs, and an equal-weight ensemble lifts a global factor portfolio's out-of-sample Sharpe by ~30%. The momentum paper finds per-market nonlinear fits give a "marginal but robust" improvement over a global nonlinear model despite far smaller per-market sample sizes, over a 30+ year walk-forward.

Why a quant firm should care: A named systematic manager publishing walk-forward Sharpe numbers, including explicit net-of-cost turnover analysis, for seasonality and TSMOM variants — directly in-domain, and a clean real-world case study of the bias-variance tradeoff in local versus global model fitting.

03Fine-tuning an order-flow foundation model for sub-second forecastingarXiv 2611.14117, independent researcher (Fedor Shabashev), Nov 10, 2026

What it is: Pretrains a 300M-parameter autoregressive model on Level-3 (full order-by-order) crypto order-flow data, then fine-tunes it to forecast microprice return and realised volatility at 200ms–30s horizons.

Result: Beats gradient-boosting-on-handcrafted-features and GARCH baselines at 200–500ms direction and volatility prediction; fine-tuned predictive ability transfers zero-shot to assets never fine-tuned on. The paper is explicit that predicted moves are smaller than spread-plus-fees, so it frames the application as market-making/execution support, not directional alpha — a useful credibility signal.

Why a quant firm should care: Directly relevant to market-making and execution research, with a genuine zero-shot cross-asset transfer result for a non-language sequential modality.

04Deep learning for operator maps in optimal liquidationarXiv 2611.14245, King's College London (Mathematics) + Decision Science Limited, London, Nov 10, 2026

What it is: A neural-network surrogate for the solution map of an optimal-execution problem under a general transient price-impact kernel, trained once to avoid re-solving the control problem from scratch for each new kernel specification.

Result: Shows the optimal liquidation strategy is locally Lipschitz-continuous in the model's parameters, justifying the learned-operator approach; uses the trained network to quantify the financial cost of misspecifying the impact kernel — directly useful for an execution desk's model-risk budget.

05Benchmarking AI risk behaviors ("Risk Toolbox")arXiv 2611.16318, Luhan Mikaelson, Robert Adragna, Emmett Brosowsky & Hayley Clatterbuck, Nov 12–13, 2026

What it is: A benchmark measuring how risk-averse versus risk-tolerant LLMs are, and how stable that risk propensity is across prompt framing, financial-vs-non-financial stakes, self-vs-other decisions, and hypothetical-vs-real-incentive conditions.

Result: 2026-generation frontier models are more stable, more consistent, and more risk-neutral than older models, increasingly defaulting to expected-value maximisation — a pattern that holds broadly across frontier models except one unusually risk-averse, domain-sensitive outlier.

Why a quant firm should care: Unusually directly relevant — if you're deploying LLM-based agents anywhere near trading or capital-affecting decisions, their built-in risk propensity, and whether it survives prompt rewording, is exactly the property you'd want benchmarked before granting any autonomy.

06Trait hitchhiking in LLM post-trainingarXiv 2611.14683, University of Cambridge (Applied Mathematics and Theoretical Physics), Nov 10, 2026

What it is: Derives the Price equation (borrowed from evolutionary biology, which describes how traits linked to a selected trait "hitchhike" along with it) for policy-gradient RL, formalising how RL post-training can silently amplify or suppress behavioural traits the reward signal never explicitly targets.

Result: Empirically validated on GRPO-trained language models — the Price-equation estimate closely tracks the actual sampled trait dynamics observed during training.

Why it's interesting: A genuinely novel cross-pollination giving a predictive/diagnostic tool for unintended behavioural drift during RL fine-tuning — relevant to anyone RL-training an in-house research or trading-support model, since it offers a way to detect when optimising for one reward silently shifts unrelated behaviours (risk tolerance, confidence calibration) that were never in the reward function at all.

07Coverage collapse and reference resets in policy optimisationarXiv 2611.14467, institution not stated on the fetched abstract excerpt, Nov 10, 2026

What it is: A theoretical analysis of why REINFORCE-family algorithms (the basis of PPO and GRPO) can fail even when the base model already samples near-optimal responses — "coverage collapse," where the probability of optimal responses is driven toward zero even as measured reward improves.

Result: Proposes "reference resets" (repeatedly resetting the KL-regularisation reference to the current policy, formalised as Approximate Policy Mirror Descent) and proves the first global convergence guarantee for a practical, coverage-preserving post-training algorithm class under this framework.

Why it's interesting: Directly actionable for anyone running GRPO/PPO post-training — explains a real, previously under-formalised failure mode with a concrete, theoretically-backed fix rather than an empirical trick.

08Interpretability is not model-specificarXiv 2611.12675, University of Houston (Computer Science), Nov 9, 2026

What it is: Shows interpretability tools found inside one LLM (a refusal direction, a correctness probe, a sentiment probe) transfer to a different LLM via a linear map between the two models' residual streams, fit only on unlabelled prompts.

Result: Tested across 110 model pairs spanning 5 families, 1B–72B parameters; a map fit on generic prompts recovers 54% of a target model's refusal-steering effect and 16–77% of its correctness-probe gain, rising to 102% and 88–98% with unlabelled prompts from the tool's own operating region, while control directions recover ≤1%.

Why it's interesting: Safety/interpretability tooling (refusal directions, steering vectors, truthfulness probes) built on one model doesn't need to be rebuilt from scratch for every new release — relevant to any team maintaining interpretability or safety infrastructure across model upgrades, including internal fine-tuned research or trading LLMs.

09MicroLens: read, trace and rewire the residual stream with two lensesarXiv 2611.13322, Stanford University + one independent researcher, Nov 9, 2026

What it is: An interactive interpretability tool that decodes every residual-stream cell with both a standard "logit lens" and a new "Jacobian lens," traces which earlier tokens a cell's information actually came from, and lets a researcher causally edit activations with the rest of the model rerun live.

Result: On Gemma-3-4B-it, two-hop trivia shows an intermediate entity is decodable from layer 7 onward and causally redirects the final answer 92–98% of the time when patched — but for simple retrieval questions, the correct answer is often already decodable before any chain-of-thought is written (89–92% of the time), except for ordering puzzles, where the model only "works it out" once it writes its reasoning.

Why it's interesting: Directly relevant to the "is chain-of-thought load-bearing or decorative?" debate, with task-dependent answers — good context for anyone evaluating how much to trust a reasoning model's visible chain-of-thought as an honest account of its computation, including in trading-signal-generation agents.

Starting Out

A closing section for anyone newer to machine learning or trading who wants this week's harder ideas explained in plain English — no prior background assumed. If you already know these, the rest of the edition is written for you too; this is just the on-ramp.

New to ML · Formal methods
AI-assisted formal verification at scale

"Formal verification" means mathematically proving that a piece of software does exactly what it's supposed to do, for every possible input, rather than just testing it against a sample of cases. It's the gold standard for trusting critical code, but historically far too slow and expensive to apply to anything large and real-world — usually reserved for tiny, isolated components. This week, DeepMind's AlphaProof Code used teams of AI agents to generate more than six million lines of formal proof code verifying key security properties of the actual, real-world implementation of the Signal messaging protocol. The job took four weeks; a human-only effort of this scope has historically taken years, when attempted at all. Only about 1% of the codebase needed a human to double-check it.

Important caveat: This verifies that the implementation matches a formal specification of what it's supposed to do — it doesn't prove the specification itself captures every real-world security requirement, and formally verified code has still occasionally shipped with bugs outside the verified scope in the broader industry's experience. The achievement is the scale and speed, not a claim that formal verification now makes software perfectly safe.

Think of it like this: instead of hiring an army of inspectors to spot-check a few thousand bricks on a wall, you get a mathematical guarantee that every single brick in the entire wall is exactly the right shape — and you got that guarantee in weeks instead of years.
New to ML · AI safety
Automated red-teaming: using AI to attack AI

"Red-teaming" is the practice of deliberately trying to break a system — find its jailbreaks, its security holes, the prompts that make it misbehave — before someone else does it for real. It has traditionally been slow, expert-driven human work. This week Anthropic disclosed "Anvil," a system that automates red-teaming using AI agents rather than human testers, and reported it outperforming human red-teamers on several fronts: finding a universal Chrome/Firefox browser jailbreak, finding a dangerous biological-information jailbreak in 24–48 hours versus weeks or months for human experts, and finding a leak in an anti-distillation safeguard in 1.2 hours versus 27.9 hours for expert red-teamers.

Why it matters: As models get more capable, the attack surface humans need to check grows faster than the pool of expert human red-teamers can keep up with — automated red-teaming is quickly becoming necessary just to keep pace, not merely a nice-to-have efficiency gain.

Think of it like this: instead of hiring more and more locksmiths to test whether your new lock can be picked, you build a robot locksmith that tries every pick in the world, all night, faster than any human could.
New to ML · Post-training theory
Trait hitchhiking: how RL training can change things you never asked it to

When you fine-tune a model with reinforcement learning to be better at one specific thing — say, more accurate at maths — you're reshaping which of its responses get reinforced. This week's Cambridge paper borrows a 50-year-old idea from evolutionary biology (the "Price equation," originally used to explain how a gene's neighbours on a chromosome can spread along with it even if they do nothing useful themselves) and applies the same mathematics to RL-trained language models. The finding: traits that happen to be statistically correlated with the behaviour you're rewarding can get amplified or suppressed as a side effect, purely by association, even though your reward signal never mentioned them at all.

Why it matters: This gives researchers a predictive, measurable way to flag "my reward function might be quietly changing something I never intended" before it shows up as a surprise in deployment.

Think of it like this: if you reward a dog only for sitting quickly, it might also start panting more — not because panting earns a treat, but because sitting quickly happens to make it pant, and that side effect rides along for free.
New to ML · Interpretability
Cross-model interpretability transfer

Interpretability researchers build tools that find specific, meaningful directions inside a model's internal representations — a "refusal direction" that controls whether it declines a request, a "correctness probe" that predicts whether its answer is right. Normally, every new model release means rebuilding these tools from scratch, because each model's internal geometry is different. This week's paper found that a simple linear map, fit using only ordinary unlabelled prompts, can translate a tool built for one model so it works on a completely different model — recovering the majority of its effectiveness, and nearly all of it with a small amount of extra calibration.

Why it matters: Safety and interpretability infrastructure stops being a sunk cost that resets with every model upgrade — it becomes something that can be carried forward and cheaply adapted instead.

Think of it like this: instead of learning a new city's entire street layout from scratch every time you move house, you learn a simple translation rule between the old map and the new one, and most of your old directions still work.

Trading, explained: Alternative Data

Part of an ongoing series working through trading concepts from first principles. So far: Market Making, Order Books & Microstructure, Statistical Arbitrage, Alpha & Signals, Execution, Risk, Derivatives, and now Alternative Data. Next time: Backtesting.

What it is: Alternative data ("alt-data") is any dataset outside traditional financial statements, prices and economic releases that might contain predictive information about an asset's future price or a company's fundamentals — satellite imagery of retailer parking lots, credit-card transaction panels, shipping and supply-chain tracking, web-scraped pricing and job postings, app-usage and sentiment data, and increasingly, large text/image corpora processed by ML models.

How it works: Raw alt-data is almost never directly usable — it has to be cleaned, matched to the right company/ticker (itself a hard entity-resolution problem), checked for survivorship and reporting-lag biases, and transformed into a signal with a known, testable relationship to a trading outcome. The real edge in alt-data is rarely the raw data itself (which vendors increasingly sell to everyone) but the speed and sophistication of the pipeline that cleans it, and the discipline to prove the resulting signal survives transaction costs and doesn't just fit noise.

A simple example: A fund buys satellite imagery of a retailer's parking lots across thousands of stores. By counting cars over time and comparing to the prior year, it builds an early estimate of same-store sales growth — potentially weeks ahead of the company's own earnings report. The edge decays as more funds buy the same dataset, which is why alt-data strategies have a shorter shelf life than classical factor strategies and require continuous sourcing of new, less-crowded datasets.

Where ML fits in: ML is now central to almost every stage of the alt-data pipeline: computer vision for satellite/geospatial imagery, NLP and large language models for parsing unstructured text (news, filings, job postings, social media) into structured signals, and entity-resolution models for matching messy real-world records to the right company. This week's order-flow foundation-model paper (Section 05) is itself a form of alt-data research applied to market microstructure rather than fundamentals — the same "pretrain on a large raw signal, then fine-tune for a specific forecast" recipe increasingly shows up across both domains.
